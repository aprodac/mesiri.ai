1 · What a construction company is, and how it makes money

A business that buys, builds and bills, on thin margins

A construction company turns money, material and labour into a building, usually for someone else, and gets paid in stages as the work is measured. Most of its spending happens before it is paid for that work, and most of its profit can disappear in small leaks along the way.

The kinds of companies

KindWhat they doTypical customer for Mesiri?
Building contractorBuilds for a client (a house owner, a company, a developer) under a contract.Yes: our main buyer. Several sites at once, 5 to 50 engineers.
Builder / developerBuys land, builds flats or villas, sells them. Is its own client.Yes for construction; their sales and bookings side is a different product.
Government contractorRoads, buildings for public bodies; tenders, strict billing and material rules.Later: more paperwork, longer sales.
Specialist / subcontractorOne trade: electrical, plumbing, plastering, waterproofing.Sometimes; often they are our customer's suppliers.

How the contract decides who carries the risk

Contract typeHow the contractor is paidWho carries the risk of cost overrun
Item rate (BOQ)A rate per unit of each work item (₹ per m³ of concrete, ₹ per sq ft of plaster), paid on measured quantity.Contractor carries cost per unit; client carries quantity changes.
Lump sumA fixed price for the whole job, paid in stages.Contractor carries almost all of it.
Labour onlyThe client buys material; the contractor supplies labour.Contractor's risk is labour productivity; material must be reconciled with the client.
Cost plusActual cost plus a fee or percentage.Client; the contractor must prove every cost.

Where the money goes

Materialoften 40–60%
Labouroften 20–30%
Subcontractvaries widely
Equipment & transporta few %
Site & office overheadsa few %
What is left (margin)often single digits to low teens

Rough ranges for building work; every company and project differs. The point that matters: when the margin is around 10%, losing 3% of material is losing about a quarter of the profit.

2 · The life of a project

From winning the job to the end of the defects period

PhaseWhat happensWhere profit is won or lost
1 WinDrawings become a BOQ (bill of quantities): every work item with quantity and rate. The price is negotiated or bid.A rate set too low loses money before the first brick.
2 Set upThe estimate becomes the budget; team, suppliers and subcontractors are lined up; the site is prepared.A budget nobody tracks against is just a document.
3 ExecuteDaily: work done, labour, material in and out, issues. Most of the money is spent here.Leaks of material, idle labour, rework and delays.
4 Bill and collectWork done is measured and billed to the client as running-account (RA) bills, usually monthly.Late or under-measured bills starve cash; disputes delay payment.
5 Hand overSnags fixed, final bill, handover, closeout.Unbilled extras (variations) are forgotten; leftover stock is lost.
6 Defects periodOften around a year in which the contractor fixes defects; part of each bill (retention) is held until it ends.Retention never chased is money given away.

5 · How money flows

Money goes out every day; it comes in once a month, late

  • The cash gap. Labour is paid weekly and material often before the client pays for the work it went into. A contractor funds the work for one or two months, with its own money, supplier credit or a bank overdraft.
  • Retention. The client often holds back a share of each bill (commonly around 5%) until the defects period ends. It is real money owed and easy to forget.
  • Advances. Some clients pay a mobilisation advance, recovered from later bills; contractors often give advances to labour contractors and subcontractors too. Every advance must be tracked until it is recovered.
  • Why bills matter so much. Every week an RA bill is late, or under-measured, is a week the company finances the client's building.

7 · The information problem

Everything is recorded somewhere; nothing is known in time

A typical mid-size contractor already has the information. It is just in the wrong places, in the wrong form, too late:

Where it lives todayWhat goes wrong
Paper registers on site (attendance, stock, deliveries)Reach the office days later, if ever; can't be added up across sites.
WhatsApp chats and photosEverything is there, and nothing can be found or totalled.
Excel (estimates, bills, stock sheets)One person's file, many versions, not linked to anything else.
Tally (the CA's books)Accurate but after the fact, and by account, not by project or work item.
Phone callsThe MD calls the engineer to ask what happened; the answer is from memory.
The result: decisions are made on old information or none. Problems are found at month end, at closeout, or by the CA, when nothing can be done about them. What the GM asked for is the opposite: know the same day, while it can still be fixed.

9 · One load of cement through the whole business

Follow 200 bags and you have seen every module

  1. Planning. The BOQ has 120 m³ of M20 concrete. At roughly 8 bags per m³, the project needs about 960 bags of cement, budgeted at ₹400 a bag.
  2. Site. The engineer sees the first-floor slab is due next Friday and only 30 bags are left.
  3. Materials. A request: 200 bags by Friday, for the first-floor slab.
  4. Procurement. Purchase orders 200 bags from Malabar Hardware at ₹400 + GST. A warning shows that nobody else has ordered cement this week.
  5. Procurement / Materials. Tuesday, the lorry brings 200; 3 bags are torn and rejected. 197 accepted on the GRN; stock rises.
  6. Site / Labour. Friday, 4 masons and 6 helpers pour 24 m³. The engineer reports the work and 200 bags used.
  7. Cost control. 24 m³ should have used about 192 bags; 200 were used: 4% over, against a 2% allowance. Amber. Labour cost for the pour is within budget.
  8. Procurement / Finance. Malabar's bill says 200 bags at ₹420. It doesn't match the order (₹400) or the delivery (197): flagged, corrected to 197 × ₹400, then paid on the due date.
  9. Accounts. The payment lands in Malabar's ledger and in the villa's project cost.
  10. Client billing. At month end the 24 m³ is measured and billed to the client at the BOQ rate, minus 5% retention.
  11. Owner's view. The villa's profit so far, cash in and out, and the one amber item to look at.
Without connected modules, each of these steps happens in a different notebook, file or person's head, and nobody can see that the cement was 4% over, the bill was ₹5,200 too high, and 3 bags never arrived.

12 · Key terms

BOQ
Bill of quantities: every work item with quantity and rate; the project's price and budget.
RA bill
Running-account bill: a stage bill to the client for work measured so far.
Retention
A share of each bill held back until the defects period ends.
Defects period
The time after handover when the contractor must fix defects.
Variation
Work added or changed by the client, priced and approved separately.
Mobilisation advance
Money paid by the client at the start, recovered from later bills.
DPR
Daily progress report from a site.
QS
Quantity surveyor: measures work, prepares and checks bills and costs.
GRN
Goods receipt note: the record of material accepted at site.
Margin
What is left of the contract value after all costs.
